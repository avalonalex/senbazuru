The material and holds are symmetric between the two halves. They are free to separate: touching vertices on different layers are distinct, with no equations welding them together. Only the three vertices on the real crease are shared. These symmetric controls settle to almost coincident surfaces.

“Passed” requires convergence, relative edge-length error ≤ 1e-5, unchanged holds and material, crease error ≤ 1e-5 radians, the whole-sheet contact check at its existing 1e-7 distance tolerance, and nonnegative exact lower/upper gaps. Matching material positions still change with refinement: two resolutions do not establish a final, mesh-independent shape.

How can both sides move without crossing?

Every overlap between a lower and upper triangle is checked at its corners, using exact fractions of the stored coordinates. The contact derivative includes both surfaces and the movement of the overlap corner itself. A shared crease vertex has one identity, so its contributions cancel instead of tearing the connection.

After a proposed step, the overlaps are measured again. If any gap is negative, the repair moves free upper vertices upward and free lower vertices downward by the same smallest common amount. Held vertices stay fixed, and rounding goes outward on each side. The material energy is measured after repair, and the stored mesh must have no negative gap.

This repair is an explicit numerical choice, not paper thickness or a physical deformation law. The fixed-lower baseline uses the earlier one-sided repair. Both approaches retain the original material objective and acceptance tolerances. Arbitrary folding directions, changing layer orders and a general crane-body opening remain outside this small experiment.
