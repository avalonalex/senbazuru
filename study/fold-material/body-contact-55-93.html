Where are 55 and 93?

The saved layer order puts 93 below 55. This comes from the original paper panels, not the triangle numbers or this camera. A gap measures triangle 55's height minus triangle 93's height at a corner of their top-view overlap. A negative gap means that local order is reversed.

The blue vertex and the green overlap corners

Vertex 30 belongs to triangle 55. Its top-view position is outside triangle 93's outline. The crossing test measures its perpendicular distance to triangle 93's extended plane: imagine that triangle's flat surface continuing beyond its edges. The sign follows its written vertex order.

The six overlap corners lie in the actual top-view overlap. Their height gaps and vertex 30's extended-plane distance measure different places. The gaps stay within the existing 1e-7 sheet-unit tolerance, but the refused trial's vertex distance reaches about −1.542e-7. Both triangles then have vertices beyond ±1e-7 of the other's plane, and their intersection is long enough to flag a crossing.

Would an overlap guard see the loss?

A guard is a local prediction used when choosing a correction direction. Here we only evaluate hypothetical guards along the saved direction. Each guard asks a nonnegative starting gap to stay nonnegative, or an already negative gap to get no worse. This is stricter than the final geometry check's 1e-7 tolerance. The derivative includes the movement of both triangles and of their overlap corner.

At corner 4, the gap changes from +8.466e-8 to −2.092e-8 at 1/64, almost exactly as predicted. At corner 5, an already negative gap worsens slightly in both fractions: this also fails a “get no worse” guard, even though the existing geometry test accepts 1/128. The labels 4 and 5 in the enlarged drawings refer to these table rows.

A failed prediction means the old direction would not satisfy that extra guard. It does not prove that solving again with the guard would produce a useful direction, or that a resulting trial would pass the actual geometry checks.
