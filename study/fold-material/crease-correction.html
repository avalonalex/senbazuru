Can contact correction untangle one crease?

A small bending sheet gives us a precise answer. Contact forces reduce penetration by millions of times and the numerical checks pass. A tiny negative gap remains—even when the two layers start in perfect contact.

Keep one panel still, let the other settle

The lower panel stays at its known bent shape. The outer quarter of the upper panel is held against it. Between those holds, the upper paper can move in all three directions. The panels share one crease: its vertices stay fixed, but its angle has a flexible preference for 180° (closed). Bending inside each panel prefers flat paper.

Why can a converged result still penetrate?

The solver assigns a large but finite cost to penetration. It can trade a tiny amount of penetration against bending and length costs. At zero gap that quadratic contact cost has zero force; a small negative gap supplies a balancing force. Here even the perfectly touching start develops the same residual, so the result is not simply a leftover from the penetrating input. This is a repeatable test for stronger contact constraints.

The reference measurement clips each upper triangle over the fixed lower panel and evaluates the height difference at every resulting corner. Rational arithmetic preserves the exact values of the stored coordinates. It checks order against this lower panel; the separate whole-mesh checker covers other contact at its numerical tolerance.
