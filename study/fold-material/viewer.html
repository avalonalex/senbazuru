This is a geometry study, not a physics simulation. The solver corrects lengths and layer order in an already posed sheet. It does not determine crease angles or panel bending stiffness, and it treats paper as having zero thickness. Passing these checks is not proof of physical feasibility.

What this experiment measures

Kite and blintz are authored rigid cases: their folding states supply angles to the existing folding engine. Each angle list includes every source edge, and the largest panel stays still. Material indices are shared across creases while lighting normals remain separate. These cases have no length-correction iterations or general contact check. The remaining details describe the original single/double controls.

The length comparison starts with the sharp examples. A coordinated least-squares correction reduces edge-length errors and penalises reversed layers. A line search keeps each accepted iteration from increasing that combined objective. The iteration selector displays recorded solutions, never interpolated vertex positions. A zero-iteration single fold is expected: its rigid panels already preserve lengths.

For the before/after comparison, strain is measured from each triangle's original material coordinates and current positions. Its two principal strains describe the most compressed and most extended directions. The color uses whichever has larger magnitude; the cards still report actual mesh edges. The contact constraint keeps this nearly flat packet in its prescribed layer order; bending energy remains unsolved.

The packet check compares the heights of triangles wherever their footprints overlap, using the expected bottom-to-top layer order. A lower layer rising above an upper one is an ordering violation. Length correction alone produced an ochre patch at the crease intersection where layers crossed. The coupled correction also penalises those crossings. This check applies to these nearly flat packets only: it does not check folds within a panel or motion between iterations. Once the packet check passes, the viewer breaks almost-equal depth ties using that same layer order, reversing it when viewed from underneath. This only adjusts display depth, not the mesh positions. States that fail the check receive no tie-break, so their crossings remain visible. Solver checkpoints have no SVG preview because the current SVG painter assumes a fixed layer order and would hide crossings in unfinished iterations.

The sharp single fold rotates one rigid half around its crease. The sharp double fold uses gaps that close at the creases and grow towards the free edges. Two of the panels bend into gently curved surfaces. That captures a drawing target, but their measured extension means the shape is not an unstretched square.

The rounded single fold consumes a strip of width πr, with r = 0.015. The second rounded bend uses the same strip for radii r and 3r, making the outer strip three times as long: 200% extension. Its layer separation is exaggerated to 3% of the original sheet's side. The sharp example instead specifies a 2.5% free-edge gap; that gap is not paper thickness.

Edge extension compares each mesh edge's 3D length with its original length. Chords are slightly shorter than circular arcs, producing the rounded single fold's small compression. For the original sharp and rounded examples, the heat map shows analytic local extension at each triangle's material centroid: maximum over directions for sharp panels, and along the second fold for rounded bends. The color ranges differ between shapes and are printed below the view.

All measurements and model positions come from Haskell. Shared vertex indices connect the surface; the viewer splits lighting normals at sharp creases without splitting the material. Connectivity and length checks alone do not establish contact or physical feasibility.
